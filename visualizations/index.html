Flock by Block

An exploration of eBird observations to follow four species across the city and ask why each one is where it is.

Concept, analysis, design, and development by Alina Scantlebury

San Francisco has over 750 species of birds.
Most people walk past them every day.

Part One

The Crow and Jay

Clement Street is known as one of the best streets for restaurants in San Francisco. Living here and walking it every day, you start to notice things. The crows know exactly where to be.

They are not here by accident. They are here because we are here — because we leave things behind, because we fill bins and forget lids and eat outside. The crow has figured us out.

But then I started wondering whether other birds had done the same thing. Steller's Jays were an easy place to look. Walk away from the restaurants, toward the quieter residential blocks, and there they are — in the backyard trees, on the fence posts, nowhere near a bin. The jay has figured out a completely different city than the crow has.

Same family. Same neighborhood. Completely different strategy.

Crow and Jay Sighting Frequency by Neighborhood Commercial Density
Average sighting frequency in SF neighborhoods with the fewest vs most food businesses.

Crows are urban generalists. Their sighting frequency barely shifts between SF's quietest residential neighborhoods and its densest commercial corridors, 37% to 35%. They don't need Clement Street. They just don't avoid it.

That's because food businesses create exactly the infrastructure crows exploit: dumpsters, outdoor dining scraps, bin collection days. The same density that drives jays out — noise, foot traffic, reduced canopy — functions as a food subsidy for crows. They are commensal.

Jays are the opposite. Their frequency drops 2.5x in high-commercial neighborhoods. Same family, opposite strategy. That contrast is what this chapter is about.

Part Two

The Parrot

If you saw the 2003 documentary, you know the parrots live on Telegraph Hill. They don't anymore. Or rather — they do, a little. But something shifted around 2013 and the colony found somewhere it liked better.

Fort Mason. Open grass, bay views, fewer people asking them to perform. By 2022 Fort Mason had 92 sightings. Telegraph Hill had 30.

By 2025 the Presidio had become the fastest-growing site, peaking at 68 sightings. The colony's center of mass has shifted west. Drag the slider to watch it happen.

Part Three

The Hawk

The Golden Gate is a bottleneck. Every hawk, falcon, and kite moving south along the Pacific coast has to cross the bay somewhere. Most of them cross here. Most residents never look up.

In September and October alone: 29% of all annual raptor sightings in SF County. Two months. The rest of the year shares the other 71%.

Methodology

The primary dataset is the eBird Basic Dataset for San Francisco County (US-CA-075), downloaded from the Cornell Lab of Ornithology. The raw file contained 5,054,584 rows cleaned to 621,286 approved, deduplicated records covering 757 species. The cleaning pipeline was written in Python using pandas with assistance from Claude (Anthropic).

One secondary dataset from DataSF provided urban context. The Registered Businesses dataset was filtered to active food establishments using NAICS and LIC code keywords. A three-pass pipeline with manual name mapping was required to fill all 111 SF analysis neighborhoods.

All visualizations are built in D3.js v7 and embedded as iframe elements. The animated parrot bubble map uses Leaflet.js for the basemap with a D3 SVG overlay. Synchronized hover between the radial bar chart and small multiples uses the postMessage API.
